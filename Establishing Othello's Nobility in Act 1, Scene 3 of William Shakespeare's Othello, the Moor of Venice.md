# Establishing Othello's Nobility in Act 1, Scene 3 of William Shakespeare's Othello, the Moor of Venice

[[Othello]] must have a high position to fall from as a [[tragic hero]], but his high position must not keep him from being likable.  The audience must be able to recognize his humanity, and feel sympathy for him, in order to experience the [[catharsis]] a tragedy offers.  When Othello is forced to defend himself before the duke and senators against [[Brabantio]]’s accusation that he uses witchcraft to seduce [[Desdemona]], literary techniques, specifically [[diction]], [[syntax]], repetition, and [[anaphora]] characterize Othello as noble: a humble general worthy of both admiration and sympathy.
Syntax characterizes Othello as humble despite being a great general. The way he forms his sentences takes the weight off of himself, take for instance,  “Rude am I in my speech.” (Shakespeare 1.3.82) The placement of “I” after the word “rude” emphasizes the value of Othello. Not only does Othello deemphasize himself in sentences by shifting “I”, but he also uses [[epistrophe|epistrophes]] to remove himself from the action, for example: “Her father lov’d me, oft invited me; Still question’d me the story of my life.” (Shakespeare 1.3.129) The use of epistrophes places the blame for the action onto Desdemona’s father. Finally and most basic of all, Othello knows what he can speak to,“[to] little of this great world can I speak / More than pertains to feats of broil and battle[.]” (Shakespeare 1.3.87-88) The use of no fancy technique and purely humbling himself into a self-degrading allows Othello to prove to others that he is not only a worthy general, but he is a humble man knowing of his limits and shortcomings. 
	Repetition further characterizes Othello as one to be admired rather than demonized. Othello uses repetition to communicate the vast number of encounters he has had, with the court he says: “Wherein I spoke [...] / Of moving accidents[...] / Off hair-breadth scapes [...] / Of being taken [...] / And sold to slavery [...] / And portance in my travel’s history; / Wherein of antres vast.” (Shakespeare 1.3.134-141) The repetition of “And” and “Of” allows Othello to pile on more and more and more events, showing his admiral past. Othello also uses his repetition of “Of” to communicate his love for Desdemona, saying “[o]f my whole course of love – what drugs, what charms, / What conjuration, and what mighty magic / I won his daughter.” (Shakespeare 1.3.92-95) The sudden shift from “what” to the final line, “I won his daughter,” brings attention to the line and emphasizes the line. Finally, Othello uses repetition to prove his and Desdemona’s love, “[s]he lov’d me for the dangers I had pass’d / And I lov’d her that she did pity them. / This is the witchcraft I have us’d” (Shakespeare 1.3.169-171) The repetition and switching of “she lov’d me” to “I lov’d her” brings attention to the couples love, additionally the repeated line “This is the witchcraft I have us’d” ends Othello’s defense. 
	Diction and anaphora characterize Othello as a man worthy of sympathy. Othello uses language that portrays himself as pitiful, he says “[f]or since these arms of mine had seven years’ pith, / Till now some nine moons wasted, they have us’d / Their dearest action in the tented field[.]” (Shakespeare 1.3.83-86) The use of age allows the listener to envision a 7-year-old child being raised on the battlefield, which creates a mood that portrays Othello as a man who deserves pity. Othello depicts himself as a man of God, he says he “Took once a pliant hour, and found good means / To draw from her a prayer of earnest heart / That I would all my pilgrimage dilate[.]” (Shakespeare 1.3.154-156) The use of Christian imagery such as in the words pilgrimage and prayer shows that Othello is a deeply religious man, this garters sympathy in the eyes of the council, who are in themselves religious men. Finally, Othello uses repetition to conclude, his “story being done, / She gave me for my pains a world of sighs; / She swore, in faith ‘twas strange, ‘twas passing strange; / ‘Twas pitiful, ‘twas wondrous pitiful.” (Shakespeare 1.3.160-163) The repeating ‘twas emphasizes the pitiful nature of his statements.   
	Literary techniques, specifically diction, syntax, repetition, and anaphora characterize Othello as noble.  He is humble in relating his nearly unbelievable successes in love and war without seeming to be an aggressive barbarian, allowing the audience to feel both sympathy and admiration for all he has experienced. Establishing this allows the audience to sympathize with Othello and feel catharsis at the end of the play when his tragic flaw brings about his downfall, conveying the theme that even great men are vulnerable to disaster.
