# Line of Inquiry - How Does August Wilson Utilize the Concept of Relationships and the Responsibilities Inherent in Them to Characterize Troy

Throughout the novel, [[August Wilson]] utilizes the idea of [[responsibility]] and happiness in relations in order to characterize the main cast. In the beginning of the play we see how [[Troy Maxson|Troy]] has a focus on how he needs to provide for his family. We hear this when [[Cory]] asks him why he doesn’t buy a TV, Troy responds with “[w]hile you thinking about a TV, I got to be thinking about the roof … and whatever else go wrong around here” (Wilson 1.3.32). Troy shoots down Cory’s request by bringing up an important concept for the remainder of the play, the idea of responsibility for and in the family. We see this idea again presented in scene 3 of act 1 during Troy and Cory’s fight. When Cory asks his father why he doesn’t like him, Troy responds with great distress that he would be taking care of Cory because of a liking, he says that “[i]t’s my job. It’s my responsibility! You understand that? A man got to take care of his family” (1.3.38). We later see the idea of relationships brought up during a conversation between Troy and [[Bono]] regarding finishing the fence. In that conversation, Bono makes a deal with Troy that when the fence, another motif, is finished he’ll make his wife happy by buying a fridge, Bono promises this by saying “[t]ell you what I’ll do … when you finish building this fence for [[Rose]] … I’ll buy Lucille that refrigerator” (2.1.64). We finally see a more selfish take on the idea of being happy in a relationship. During the argument between Tory and Rose after Troy reveals his affair with Alberta he tells Rose, “I can sit up in her house and laugh. Do you understand what I’m saying. I can laugh out loud … and it feels good” (2.1.69). This more selfish take on the idea provides a good parallel to the previous examples when examining Troy’s character. To summarize, August Wilson uses the concept of relationships to characterize Troy as a character who is at first a family man and then deeper down a man focused on his own values and wants.

Work Cited
Wilson, August. [[Fences]]. New York, New York, Plume, 1986.
