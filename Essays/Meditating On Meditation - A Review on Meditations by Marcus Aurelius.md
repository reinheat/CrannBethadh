[[Marcus Aurelius]] says that “[p]eople who labor all their lives but have no purpose to direct every thought and impulse toward are wasting their time—even when hard at work” (Aurelius 2.7). This is but one of the ideas proposed in Ta Eis Heauton, meaning “Things Unto Himself,” or by the more modern name, [[Meditations]]. The words written by Aurelius were never intended to be published; they were written purely as observations of the universe and how Aurelius saw himself within it. It was only after his death that the works were found, translated, and published. Aurelius lived as a [[Roman emperor]] and is commonly referred to as the last of the [[Five Good Emperors]]. Near-constant warfare, plagues, and hardship plagued his rule. It was during the wars with the Germanic tribes that Aurelius would write Meditations as a method of self-examination, discipline, and moral improvement.

Aurelius writes on many subjects, but almost none are more prominent than the idea of Nature and the world itself. He describes how humans have a place within the natural order and how death is an inescapable part of it. To quote from the second book, “[d]on’t ever forget these things: The nature of the world. / My nature. / [...] That you are a part of nature, and no one can prevent you from speaking and acting in harmony with it, always” (2.9). He argues that humans are fundamentally alike in body and reason, and therefore to oppose one another irrationally is to act against nature itself (2.1).

Aurelius also frames death not as something to be feared, but as a natural transformation, no different than any other changes constantly occurring in the universe. We are not to fear because humans are part of nature, death is simply a return or reconfiguration of elements rather than a moral failing or source of terror. The human body is temporary and limited, but the rational mind is humanity’s defining strength. For Aurelius, this means that a person should cultivate strong internal principles. Virtues such as reason, self-control, and justice. That support one’s well-being and allows one to live in harmony with nature regardless of external hardship.
  
Nearly as prominent as nature are Aurelius’ reflections on [[Stoicism]] and self-governance. He writes that “[e]veryone gets one life. Yours is almost used up, and instead of treating yourself with respect, you have entrusted your own happiness to the souls of others” (2.6). The central takeaway of this idea is expressed most clearly when he writes:

“Nothing is more pathetic than people who run around in circles, ‘delving into the things that lie beneath’ and conducting investigations into the souls of the people around them, never realizing that all you have to do is to be attentive to the power inside you and worship it sincerely. To worship it is to keep it from being muddied with turmoil and becoming aimless and dissatisfied with nature—divine and human. What is divine deserves our respect because it is good; what is human deserves our affection because it is like us” (2.13).

To live a good life, Aurelius argues, one must attend to the power within and treat it with reverence and sincerity. This inner mind is both divine, deserving respect because it has the potential for good, and human, deserving affection because it is shared by all people.

Finally, Aurelius writes extensively on how one ought to treat others. He claims that “the sin committed out of pleasure deserves a harsher rebuke than the one committed out of pain” (2.10) and that “[i]f the community isn’t injured by it, neither am I. And if it is, anger is not the answer. Show the offender where he went wrong” (5.22). He maintains that no transgression is too great to forgive, reminding readers that humans were made “like feet, hands, and eyes, like the two rows of teeth, upper and lower. To obstruct each other is unnatural. To feel anger at someone, to turn your back on him: these are obstructions” (2.1). He also writes, “[c]hoose not to be harmed—and you won’t feel harmed. / Don’t feel harmed—and you haven’t been” (4.7).

For Aurelius, the experience of being harmed is primarily a mental state, much like fear or happiness. While such reactions are natural, they need not dominate one’s character. Instead, he urges readers to “welcome with affection what is sent by fate,” and “not to stain or disturb the spirit within him with a mess of false beliefs,” but rather to preserve it by calmly obeying reason and justice—“saying nothing untrue, doing nothing unjust” (3.16).

To conclude, Meditations presents a deeply Stoic and naturalistic view of the world. Aurelius reflects on humanity’s place in nature, the inevitability of death, and the importance of governing one’s own mind rather than fearing the actions of others. The work remains inspiring not only as philosophy but as a record of personal moral struggle. For anyone with an interest in [[philosophy]], or even a passing curiosity about how to live well among others, this book is a compelling and worthwhile read. The language and structure can be challenging however, Aurelius himself notes a lesson he learned from [[Rusticus]]: “[t]o read attentively—not to be satisfied with ‘just getting the gist of it’” (1.7). If a reader is willing to reflect carefully on what Aurelius’ words mean in their own life, Meditations offers enduring insight.

As a work, it earns a strong recommendation. However, when it comes to any attempt to revise or “improve” the ideas is a mockery. To change even one of Aurelius’ central principles would be to replace Aurelius' Meditations with one’s own Meditations, and that, ultimately, would no longer be Aurelius' things unto himself.
