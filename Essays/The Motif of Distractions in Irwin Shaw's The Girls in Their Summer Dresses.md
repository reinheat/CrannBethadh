# The Motif of Distractions in Irwin Shaw's The Girls in Their Summer Dresses

In [[Irwin Shaw]]’s “[[The Girls in Their Summer Dresses]],” the [[motif]] of distraction reveals that an “ideal couple” is truly deeply flawed.  In the beginning, [[Michael]] and [[Frances]] are characterized as a stereotypical married couple, who hold hands and walk together, but in actuality there’s tension between them. When walking down fifth avenue Michael turns his head to look at another woman and Frances says “Look out, [...] You’ll break your neck” (Shaw 59). This piece of dialog from Frances tells us that this happens enough where she’s comfortable joking about it and it characterizes Michael as someone who keeps looking at other women, even with his wife at his side. As the story progresses,  we see the veil of lies and covers start to peel away, revealing the true nature of both characters. After their breakfast Michael and Frances get drinks at a bar, causing Micheal to say “I love the way women look. [...] shopping in their furs and their crazy hats” (61). This characterizes Michael as someone who makes surface level observations of women, this even causes his wife to call him childish and suggest that he grow up. By the end, we see the full unraveling of the fabricated lies, both characters have laid bare before each other, but even with full unraveling of their characters they still maintain their fabricated [[persona|personas]]. Frances, after Michael admitted that he may sleep with another woman, “stopped crying then. Two or three snuffles into the handkerchief […] her face didn’t tell anything to anybody” (62). This action shows that Frances and by extension Michael hurt each other but what’s more important is the walls they put up to protect themselves. This final action suggests that we endure pain to hide our true vulnerabilities. 

Work Cited
