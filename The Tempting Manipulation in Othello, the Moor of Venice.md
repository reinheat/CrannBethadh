# The Tempting Manipulation in Othello, the Moor of Venice

The story begins in Venice, Italy, in 1603 when [[Othello]] marries the fair [[Desdemona]]. Afterward, Othello is summoned to the island of Cyprus to fight the oncoming Turks. This is the perfect opportunity for the “Honest” [[Iago]] to enact his revenge on the Moor of Venice. He drives Othello mad with rumors that his newlywed wife is unfaithful and sleeping with his lieutenant [[Cassio]]. Initially, Othello is skeptical, requesting visual proof of his wife’s deceit. Iago provides the optical proof by having his wife, [[Emilia]], steal a handkerchief that Othello gifted to Desdemona and having Cassio’s mistress, [[Bianca]], question him about it within view but not the auditory range of Othello. After seeing this proof, Othello vows to strangle Desdemona on the very sheets that would confirm their marriage, and Iago vows to kill Cassio. While Othello strangles Desdemona, Iago fails to kill Cassio and enters the bedchambers of Desdemona and is revealed by his wife for all his deceits. Othello realizes he murdered the love of his life over the temptations of a cunning trickster and kills himself, laying his body next to the woman he loves most. In [[William Shakespeare]]’s Othello, the Moor of Venice, the characters of Othello and Iago represent man's struggles against Others, suggesting that compromising personal values to the influences of others causes a loss in personal values.
To begin, Othello symbolically represents a virtuous and “pure” Christian man, namely Adam, while Iago represents a tempting trickster, akin to the Snake of [[Garden of Eden|Eden]], this is shown through religious diction and allusion.  Othello, when questioned about his love of Desdemona, by the Duke of Venice, says, “[t]o draw from her a prayer of earnest heart / That I would all my pilgrimage dilate” (Shakespeare 1.3.153-154). The use of religious diction through words like “prayer”, “pilgrimage”, and “earnest heart” is used to give Othello a holy and pure feeling. Moreover, towards the end of Act 3 Iago gives a soliloquy in which he says, “I will in Cassio’s lodging lose this napkin / And let him find it. [...] The Moor already changes with my poison. / Which at the first are scarce found to distaste, / But with a little act upon the blood / Burn like the mines of sulfur” (3.3.329-337). The hellish imagery and mention of poison allude to Iago being a serpent akin to the snake of [[temptation]]. Finally, Othello is infected by Iago’s venom after a short conversation, cursing his love for Desdemona with the words,  “[a]rise, black vengeance, from the hollow hell! / Yield up, O love, thy crown and hearted throne / To tyrannous hate! Swell, bosom, with thy fraught, For ‘tis of aspic’s tongues” (3.3.450-458). For the once pure and noble Othello to curse his love after speaking with Iago using hellish imagery shows corruption by the serpent. Through the use of religious diction and allusion, the corruption of Iago on Othello is hinted at.  
Additionally, the change of Othello acts as a symbolic parrel to the degradation of his values. When the story begins the Duke of Venice summons Othello for a message saying, “Valiant Othello, we must straight employ you / Against the general enemy Ottoman” (1.3.49-50). The use of the word “Valiant” immediately characterizes Othello as someone the Duke, a high-ranking voice of Venice, relies on. However, it is not long until Iago’s venom reaches Othello, at this point Othello slaps Desdemona in public to which Lodovico says, “Is this the noble Moor whom our full senate / Call all in all sufficient? [...] (IAGO) He is much changed” (4.1.259-264). The contrast between Othello who was once “valiant” and Othello who is doubted to even be sufficient displays a fall in his role, a willful abandonment for the protector of Venice. Finally, after Othello murders Desdemona he regains some of his honor by saying, “I have done the state some service, and they know’t. / No more of that. I pray you in your letters, / When you shall these unlucky deeds relate, [...] Set you down this, [...] I took by the throat the [Turk] / And smote him, thus. [Othello] Stabs himself” (5.2.355-373).  Othello, by equating himself to a Turk, denotes himself as an enemy of the state, and, by killing himself an enemy of the state, regains some of his protector status. Through the use of a symbolic parallel, the degradation of Othello and his values becomes increasingly evident.
Finally, throughout the play there is a constant idea of manipulating others, most often when this happens it causes a loss of personal values. Iago is the most obvious subject for manipulating others, for example when speaking with Othello he says, “[Desdemona] did deceive her father, marrying you, / And when she seemed to shake and fear your looks, / She loved them most” (3.3.210). Iago's suggestion that Desdemona is unfaithful and loves Othello only for his fearful monstrous traits is a use of [[dramatic irony]] as the audience knows that Desdemona is completely faithful and loyal to Othello. Iago further corrupts and manipulates Othello by using the idea of Desdemona and corrupting it, Othello hears Desdemona say something regarding Cassio to which he responds negatively, Desdemona says “[a] most unhappy one. I would do much / T’ atone them, for the love I bear to Cassio. (Othello) Fire and brimstone! [...] I am glad to see you mad. [...] (striking her) Devil!” (4.1.221-232) Here Othello invokes hellish imagery as his perception of who Desdemona is is manipulated by Iago. The use of hellish imagery for a once holy character provides a stark contrast indicating a large change in Othello’s perceptions. Iago’s [[manipulation]] goes further than just affecting Othello, he also successfully attempts to manipulate [[Roderigo]], he tells Roderigo, “[i]t cannot be long that Desdemona should continue her love to the / Moor - put money in thy purse - nor he his to her. It was / a violent commencement in her, and thou shalt see an / answerable sequestration - put but money in thy purse. / These Moors are changeable in their wills - fill thy purse / with money. [...] When she is sated with his / body she will find the errors of her choice. Therefore, put / money in thy purse” (1.3.339-349). The repetition of “fill thy purse” creates an emphasis on the idea, it suggests to Roderigo that he should do nothing but fill Iago’s purse till it becomes heavy with gold, it also suggests that the connection between Iago and Roderigo is that of a puppet and it’s master. Through the use of manipulation, the play suggests a connection between manipulation and the degrading of personal values.
In conclusion, throughout the play, characters are used to represent a struggle to maintain personal values when faced with the regard of others, which is conveyed through the ideas of the struggles of temptation and the degrading of values through the self and others. The struggles of temptation use a [[biblical allusion]] to portray the struggle of the pure-hearted Othello when met with the devilish Iago. The degrading of values through the role of self is portrayed through Othello’s change and represents the effects of the temptation faced. Finally, the degradation of values through the role of others further enforces the idea that temptation and manipulation lead to a degradation of values. These ideas combine to create a theme that places the importance of self-values onto others leading to a degradation of said values.
